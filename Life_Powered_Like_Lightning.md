## 1. Using Oxygen in an Oxygen-Free World

Europa captured the public’s imagination partly because of the mysteries hidden beneath its ice. Its ocean may contain places where warm, chemically rich water creates seafloor environments capable of supporting life, but present-day seafloor hydrothermal circulation at any particular site is not established. Europa may simply have a dark, barren ocean. We don’t even know how much usable oxygen is available there. ([Vance et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/2017JE005341); [Byrne et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-67151-3))

![LP-01 — Conceptual Europa-like under-ice environment](../figures/LP01_EUROPA_ENVIRONMENT_LEAD_FINAL_2026-09-26.png)

*Figure LP-01 — Environmental lead. Conceptual Europa-like under-ice / seafloor environment; not an observed inhabited vent habitat. No organism or K1 morphology is depicted because the project does not assign Fulgorax a canonical whole-body morphology.*

That uncertainty is at the center of this report. On Earth, microorganisms can survive in darkness by exploiting chemical reactions in their environment, and larger organisms can feed on those microbes. But what if Europa’s ocean contains little or no usable oxygen? Many anaerobic pathways extract much less usable energy from food than aerobic respiration. Instead of assuming Europa conveniently supplies the oxygen, this project asks a different question: **what if the organism made its own?**

The project gave this hypothetical organism the common name **Fulgorax**. It would split water through electrolysis, produce oxygen, and then use that oxygen for respiration. The chemistry is familiar. The problem is paying for it.

Electrolysis takes energy, and Fulgorax would have to get that energy from somewhere. The proposed source is the motion of the water itself. Flexible biological structures called **tribowhips** would extend from the organism into the surrounding flow. As seawater bends and moves them, surfaces inside each tribowhip would repeatedly make contact and separate, generating electrical charge through the same broad phenomenon responsible for static electricity.

Engineered devices already use this effect. **Triboelectric nanogenerators**, or TENGs, convert repeated motion and contact into electrical output ([Niu et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1039/C3EE42571A); [Wang et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nanoen.2021.106503)). A tribowhip would not simply be a commercial TENG shrunk down and attached to an organism. Its materials would have to be biological, it would have to repair or replace itself, survive in seawater, and connect its electrical output to metabolism.

In its simplest form, the idea is:

**moving water → electricity → oxygen → respiration**

The original Europa design, **Fulgoray**, would still have to eat. Microbial prey supplies carbon, nutrients, and reduced biological material. The electrical system solves a different problem: it provides oxygen that could allow the organism to extract more energy from that food. There is no free energy here. Fulgorax would be taking some of the mechanical energy already present in moving seawater and trying to convert it into useful biology.

None of this means a tribowhip has been demonstrated. The project identified no demonstrated organism that generates useful biological power through the proposed triboelectric pathway; the final dimensions of Fulgorax are unknown, and several of the most important steps remain experimental questions.

The idea is worth investigating because life already exploits energy from sunlight and chemistry. Fulgorax asks whether biology could exploit another resource that is common in planetary environments: **motion**.

Could life live, in part, on motion?

## 2. A Third Way to Power Life?

That question extends beyond Europa. Life can capture light through phototrophy and exploit a huge range of chemical reactions through chemotrophy. Tribotrophy proposes another possible starting point for an energy system: **mechanical motion**.

The basic process is simple to describe. A fluid moves, a biological structure moves with it, surfaces make contact and separate, and electrical charge is generated. The difficult part is turning that charge into useful biological power.

I use the word **tribotrophy** for this hypothetical strategy. The name comes from triboelectricity, where electrical charge develops through contact and separation between materials. Anyone who has walked across a carpet and then touched a metal doorknob has experienced the effect. But producing a spark is easy compared with running a metabolism. A useful biological generator has to deliver electrical energy into a real load, continue doing it cycle after cycle, and survive long enough for the energy gained to matter.

Fulgorax is one worked example of how tribotrophy might function. It is not meant to define every possible triboic organism. Different environments could favor different structures, materials, metabolisms, and ways of using the electricity.

Nor is the claim that Fulgorax exists beneath Europa’s ice. The question is more basic: **can life turn environmental motion into electricity useful enough to matter biologically?**

## 3. Where Fulgorax Came From

Fulgorax began as science fiction. While working on a hard-science-fiction world, I started wondering what kinds of energy an unfamiliar biosphere might exploit. Sunlight and chemical gradients were obvious answers, but energy is also constantly moving through an environment in physical ways.

The first example was almost embarrassingly ordinary: **wind**. Could an organism somehow live off it?

That question led to triboelectric nanogenerators. If engineers can use motion, repeated contact, and charge separation to produce electricity, could evolution produce some biological system that does something functionally similar?

The basic idea came quickly. Making it biologically believable did not.

Europa Fulgorax is the second triboic creature I have seriously designed. The earlier design explored a very different environment in the clouds of Venus. Europa forced the idea into liquid water, different mechanical loads, different materials, and a different use for the electricity. The important lesson was that tribotrophy, if it is possible at all, does not point toward one predetermined kind of creature. The environment changes the design.

The Europa project also went much deeper. Instead of carrying the earlier creature into a new setting and changing a few parts, the important assumptions were reopened and tested again.

That produced the central question behind this report:

**Can life turn the motion of its environment into electricity useful enough to matter biologically?**

A speculative creature can always be made to work if the designer is willing to keep inventing new features to rescue it. That was exactly what this project tried not to do.

## 4. Try to Kill It

A speculative creature can be made to survive almost anything if every problem is answered by inventing another feature. Too little power can be solved with more generators, too little food with a better feeding system, weak materials with tougher ones. Soon the creature loses its elegant form and becomes weighed down by adaptations the designer added simply to keep the idea alive.

This is exactly what the project tried to avoid. The question was not simply, *How can Fulgorax work?* It was, *What is the cheapest serious way to show that it cannot?* The investigation therefore started with the most important failure points. If the energy budget failed, there was no reason to spend time designing reproduction or feeding. If the electrical system could not deliver useful power, the creature was finished.

The many ways Fulgorax might fail were then explored one by one. Electrolysis might cost too much energy, or the tribowhips might produce too little useful electricity. The organism could poison itself with oxygen, starve, wear out its electrical machinery too quickly, or spend more on reproduction than the whole system could support. Even if a small Fulgorax worked, scaling might eventually break the design.

The project also avoided assigning Fulgorax one convenient metabolism and then building everything around it. Its oxygen and metabolic demands were tested across a range based on measurements from Earth organisms. Those cases gave the calculations something concrete to work with without pretending that any one of them represented the measured metabolism of a creature that does not yet exist.

Not all of the tests produced clean yes-or-no answers. Some barriers looked lower after analysis; others remained important constraints. Several reached the point where calculation alone could no longer settle them.

The electrical system became the clearest example. The obvious question at first was whether a tribowhip could generate enough charge.

The harder question was whether the organism could actually use it.

## 5. A Spark Is Not a Generator

A tribowhip generating electrical charge is not enough to declare it a success. Creating charge is only one part of the power process. That charge must go somewhere useful: it has to cross the necessary interfaces, drive a real electrical load, and do it cycle after cycle. **Generating charge is not the same thing as delivering useful power.**

This distinction became one of the most important findings of the project. How could a biological organism not only generate electricity, but actually deliver it where it was needed? Modern energy-harvesting devices called **triboelectric nanogenerators**, or TENGs, produce electrical output through repeated contact and separation, the same broad phenomenon behind static electricity ([Niu et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1039/C3EE42571A); [Zi et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms9376)). The project also modeled whether flexible tribowhips could move in the proposed seawater flows and produce enough separated charge to remain interesting. Nothing in those calculations produced an obvious reason to discard the idea.

![LP-02 / TC-01 — K1: From Motion to Useful Power?](../figures/LP02_TC01_K1_MOTION_TO_USEFUL_POWER_FINAL_2026-09-26.png)

*Figure LP-02 / TC-01 — K1: From Motion to Useful Power? Conceptual K1 schematic; not canonical anatomy. Exact spatial arrangement, material order, dimensions, polarity, gap geometry, and routing remain unresolved. EF-1 — useful transfer under a real load plus repeatable reset — has not been experimentally demonstrated.*

The source side even had one useful property. A large number of tribowhips would not all pulse at exactly the same moment. Modeling showed that an asynchronous array could smooth the combined electrical output compared with a single whip. But that only smooths the source. It does not prove that the organism receives a smooth supply of usable current.

The energy still has to be delivered.

A tribowhip could produce plenty of charge and remain useless to the organism if most of that charge leaks away, cannot cross the required interface, cannot drive the chemistry, or leaves the system unable to reset for another cycle.

No experiment has measured that complete proposed pathway, so the project did not invent an efficiency and pretend it was known. Instead, it asked a question the calculations could answer: **what fraction of the charge produced by the tribowhips would actually have to reach the biological load for Fulgorax to meet its electrical needs?**

That calculation tells us what the mechanism must achieve without claiming that it already can.

The next step is therefore an experiment, not another assumption. Build a plausible hydrated tribowhip interface, cycle it mechanically, connect it to a real load, and measure how much useful voltage and current reach that load. Then determine whether the system can reset and do it again. The speculative tribowhip architecture is detailed enough that a simplified prototype could be built for this kind of bench testing.

That experiment is **EF-1: useful electrical transfer and repeatable reset under load**. If repeated testing shows that the sealed K1 mechanism family cannot do that, the current Europa Fulgorax architecture would require redesign. Producing charge is interesting. Powering biology is what matters.

## 6. The Other Ways Fulgorax Could Die

EF-1 is the clearest unresolved electrical problem, but solving it would not prove that Fulgorax works. The creature still has to make oxygen, control the products of electrolysis, find enough food, keep its tribowhips operating, and reproduce without spending more energy and material than the system can support.

The project examined the electrical demand of splitting water and did not find an obvious catalytic barrier that immediately ended the design. That is not the same as demonstrating a biological electrolyzer. Fulgorax still needs a real biological mechanism capable of performing and controlling that chemistry.

Oxygen also creates a storage problem. One possibility was that Fulgorax would need a specialized organ to hold the oxygen it produced. Modeling suggested that may not be necessary. If oxygen is produced near the tissues or compartments that immediately use it for respiration, the organism may only need to move small quantities across short biological distances rather than maintain a large internal oxygen reserve.

Electrolysis produces hydrogen as well, and that has to go somewhere. Fulgorax could potentially release it or use it in other chemistry, but the organism cannot simply ignore it.

The mechanical system brought another set of questions. The project tested loads across different seawater speeds to see whether moving water itself would make the tribowhips or their attachment impossible. Those tests did not eliminate the design, but they also did not produce a final body shape, attachment system, or method of locomotion. The creature’s anatomy remains underdetermined.

Tribowhip durability matters for the same reason. A generator that works but wears out almost immediately may cost more to replace than it produces. The project therefore explored how different service lives change the repair and replacement burden. Reproduction was treated the same way: the organism must be able to build another viable organism without exceeding the energy and material available to it.

None of those problems supplied the clean theoretical failure the project was looking for. Several remain serious experimental questions.

Food led somewhere different.

Fulgoray began with a simple ecological assumption: it eats microbes. Its tribowhips provide electricity, the electricity helps make oxygen, and that oxygen lets the organism extract more energy from the food it captures. But that leaves Fulgoray dependent on finding enough prey in Europa’s ocean.

The project did not assume Europa conveniently contains an abundant microbial population. Feeding was tested across a range of speculative prey densities, including denser cases based on Earth-like diffuse-vent environments. Those calculations did not rule out the required food throughput in the denser comparison cases, but they also did not establish that Europa actually contains such prey densities.

More importantly, the feeding analysis raised a new question. The Fulgoray design explored whether power from a tribowhip could produce oxygen and help the organism get more energy from consumed food. But if Fulgorax can already collect substantial electrical energy, does all of that energy have to pass through prey first?

Maybe electricity could contribute to powering life in ways other than making oxygen.

That question would eventually change the creature. For the moment, it showed something useful about the design process: trying to break Fulgorax did not always eliminate an idea. Sometimes it exposed an assumption that did not need to be there.

## 7. How Small Is the Smallest Fulgoray?

When the minimum-size investigation began, I expected an answer in millimeters. Maybe there would be a minimum number of tribowhips, a minimum number of cells, or some body size that defined the lower boundary. The analysis did not support a defensible number.

The problem is that shrinking Fulgoray is not just a matter of making every part smaller. This theoretical triboic creature can take different body shapes, use different tribowhip configurations, and divide its internal functions in different ways. One design might need more surface area but less internal machinery; another could make the opposite trade. There was no single measurement that controlled the entire organism.

That changed the question. Instead of asking for the smallest body, the project asked: **what is the least biological organization capable of building, operating, repairing, and reproducing the whole system?**

Even that does not give us a clean number. The analysis did not show that Fulgoray must be multicellular, but it also did not demonstrate that one simple cell could do everything required. A sufficiently compartmentalized unicellular architecture remains possible, while more complex organization could become necessary if later experiments impose tighter constraints.

So the minimum-size study ended without a smallest Fulgoray measured in millimeters, cells, or tribowhips. That is not the same as saying there is no minimum. There must eventually be some point where the organism can no longer fit all of the machinery, material flows, repair systems, and reproductive functions it needs. The project simply did not find enough evidence to put a defensible number on that boundary.

The surviving result applies to the original heterotrophic Fulgoray architecture. The three later metabolic branches—Fulgorad, Fulgoral, and Fulgoraz—were not taken through an equivalent minimum-creature analysis, so their minimum organization remains a separate question.

The result was therefore a **conditional minimum architecture family, not a minimum size**.

## 8. Then We Tried to Make It Bigger

The maximum-size investigation began with another question that sounded as if it should produce a number: **how large could Fulgorax become?**

I expected the square-cube law to eventually impose the limit. As an organism grows, volume increases faster than surface area, creating familiar problems with structural support, transport, and exchange with the environment. It seemed reasonable that Fulgorax would eventually reach some size where those scaling problems overwhelmed the design.

But a large Fulgorax does not have to be a scaled-up version of a small one.

It could become flatter, longer, more branched, or more modular. It could distribute its tribowhips over a larger area or divide biological functions among repeated units. Each change can relieve one constraint while creating another. More tribowhip area may collect more energy, but it also puts more material into moving water. More internal machinery may support a larger organism, but that machinery also has to be built, maintained, supplied, and repaired.

Square-cube scaling never disappeared. It still matters for structural support, hydrodynamic forces, transport, and geometry. What disappeared was the idea that one scaling equation would automatically give us the maximum size. A thin, branched, surface-rich organism does not scale like an enlarged sphere, and a modular organism does not have to scale like a familiar animal.

The strongest recurring candidate backbone was the combination of K1 deployment and electrical capacity, anchoring force and moment, and durability/repair. A larger organism needs enough tribowhip capacity to support its growing energy demands, but those tribowhips also create mechanical loads and require material, attachment, repair, and replacement. Food, raw materials, and the electrical interfaces required by the different metabolic designs add more constraints.

The project could not determine which of those limits would fail first.

That means there is no defensible maximum body length, mass, or tribowhip count. It also does **not** mean Fulgorax could grow without limit. Somewhere there must be a boundary where no remaining body shape, organization, or metabolic design can satisfy all of the requirements at once. The analysis simply did not locate that boundary.

The maximum-size work therefore ended with four important limits on what can be claimed: there is no numerical maximum, there is no basis for unlimited size, no single first failure was identified, and none of the alternative metabolic branches demonstrated a larger maximum than the original Fulgoray design.

## 9. The Investigation Changed the Creature

The maximum-size work began with **Fulgoray**, the original heterotrophic branch. Fulgoray eats microbial prey and uses electricity primarily to make oxygen, allowing it to extract more energy from that food.

But the feeding problem had already exposed a broader possibility: electricity might contribute to biology in ways other than making oxygen. That became the route to three later architectures.

The first alternative, **Fulgorad**, still eats, but electricity takes over more of the metabolic work. It remains dependent on outside matter for growth and repair, but it may not need to oxidize as much prey simply to obtain energy.

**Fulgoral** pushes the idea further. Instead of relying on prey for its reduced carbon, Fulgoral would use electrical bioenergetics to help fix inorganic carbon and build biological material. That moves much more of the energy burden onto the tribowhips, but it does not make the organism independent of its environment. It still has to acquire carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, trace elements, and everything else required to build a body. Electricity can supply energy; it cannot create matter.

The final branch is **Fulgoraz**, which moves some of the difficult producer chemistry into microbial partners living inside the organism. Earth animals already depend on internal microorganisms for chemistry the host cannot easily perform alone. Fulgoraz would use the same broad division of labor in a very different system: the tribowhips provide electrical energy, the host supplies a controlled environment and raw materials, and specialized microbes perform useful chemistry and pass products back to the host.

Earth biology gives us precedents for pieces of that idea. Some microbes can take up electrons from electrodes and use that input in metabolism or carbon assimilation, including *Rhodopseudomonas palustris* TIE-1 and *Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans* ([Bose et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms4391); [Ranaivoarisoa et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-09377-6); [Ishii et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2015.00994)). Earth animals also form internal partnerships with chemoautotrophic bacteria that fix carbon and transfer useful products to their hosts ([Felbeck 1981](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.213.4505.336); [Bright et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.1007/s002270050722)). Those examples show that the component ideas are biologically relevant; they do **not** demonstrate that a complete Fulgoraz system would work.

In Fulgoraz, the internal microbes are not simply another food source. They would function as persistent metabolic partners, more like living biological machinery inside the host.

That creates its own problems. Electricity has to reach the symbionts. Nutrients have to reach them as well. Useful products have to return to the host, wastes must be controlled, and the partnership has to remain stable over time. Fulgoraz is therefore not a proven improvement over the simpler branches.

These speculative branches begin to define the possibilities for Fulgorax by exploring different ways to distribute the energy and biological burden. Fulgoray depends more heavily on prey. Fulgorad asks electricity to carry more of the energy load. Fulgoral moves producer metabolism directly into the host, while Fulgoraz moves it into an internal microbial partnership.

None of the later branches demonstrated that Fulgorax could become larger than Fulgoray. They changed which limits mattered.

The maximum-size investigation began by asking how large one creature could become. In trying to answer that question, the project discovered that there was more than one reasonable way to build the creature.

## 10. If It Existed, Would We Recognize It?

Once Fulgorax had several possible metabolic forms, another question followed: **if triboic life existed, how would we know we had found it?**

A direct search on Europa’s seafloor remains far beyond the experiments available to us now, so the project first looked at chemistry and at possible Earth analog environments.

Chemistry alone was not enough. Fulgorax could produce hydrogen. Some producer branches could release oxygen. Any living system would alter carbon chemistry and leave organic material behind. None of those signals is unique to triboic life. The project did not find a single molecule or chemical pattern that could identify a tribotroph by itself.

The machinery could be more informative than the chemistry.

If an organized biological structure were recovered and repeated movement caused it to produce electrical output under a real load, that would be far more interesting than finding hydrogen or organic carbon alone. A convincing case would still require associated local chemistry, mineral and dead-material controls, and additional biological evidence, but it would test the distinctive part of the idea directly: **whether a biological structure can harvest motion and turn it into useful electricity.**

That shifts the search for triboic life away from looking for one special molecule. The strongest evidence may be a causal process rather than a molecule.

## 11. Where the Calculations Have to Stop

The project eventually reached a limit that another calculation could not solve. Several of the largest uncertainties now depend on measurements that do not exist.

Can a hydrated tribowhip repeatedly deliver useful electricity under a real load? How quickly would its materials wear out? Can biological electrolysis be controlled well enough to supply oxygen without creating new problems? Can electrotrophic microbes use electrical output in the ways required by the later Fulgorax designs?

These are wet-lab questions.

Some simulations were deliberately left unrun because there was no useful value to put into them. A more detailed model cannot tell us the efficiency of an interface that has never been measured, or the lifetime of a biological material that has never been built. Adding more decimal places would not reduce that uncertainty.

Fortunately, the next experiments do not require discovering life on Europa. Candidate tribowhip materials can be cycled in water and tested under an electrical load. Electrolysis systems can be studied under biologically relevant conditions. Electrotrophic microbes can be grown against electrodes. Core samples can be kept under controlled flow for months.

At this stage, the project does not need a more elaborate Fulgorax. It needs measurements.

## 12. Bring Up a Rock and See What Wakes Up

The most important experiment for Fulgorax remains EF-1: build a plausible tribowhip interface, move it repeatedly in water, put it under a real electrical load, and see whether it can deliver useful power and reset for another cycle.

But there is another experiment I find just as interesting.

Go to one of Earth’s **anoxic diffuse-vent** environments and collect core material. Bring it into the laboratory and expose it for months to flowing, oxygen-free seawater with chemistry similar to the environment it came from.

Then see what grows.

If an Earth organism ever evolved a Fulgoray-like oxygen-producing triboic pathway, an anoxic environment with persistent fluid motion would be a logical place to look: mechanical energy is available while free oxygen is scarce. This rationale belongs to the oxygen-making branch, not to tribotrophy as a whole.

Finding triboic life would still be a long shot. Another—and still important—possible result is that prolonged cultivation reveals unfamiliar microbes or even previously undescribed species that short sampling and ordinary laboratory conditions miss.

The larger value is that we could watch an anoxic diffuse-vent community develop under controlled, deep-sea-like flow for months, cataloging the organisms that appear and following how the chemistry changes with them. Instead of taking a brief snapshot of the vent field, we could watch part of its ecology unfold.

And if something truly strange appears, then we ask where its energy comes from.

Bring up a rock and see what wakes up.

## 13. Life Powered Like Lightning

Fulgorax remains speculative. We still do not know whether a biological tribowhip can repeatedly deliver useful electrical power. The project did not establish an exact minimum size or maximum size, the three later metabolic branches remain conditional designs, and there is no single chemical signature that would identify triboic life.

Those are substantial gaps, but they are now much better defined than when the project began. The original question was simple: **could life somehow live off motion?**

Fulgorax gives us one detailed way to examine that question. It has an energy system that can be tested, metabolic designs that can be compared, specific places where the physics or biology may fail, and experiments that could settle some of the largest uncertainties.

Some of those experiments may kill the current design. Others may force it to change. That is normal. A speculative organism becomes scientifically useful when its weak points can be tested rather than explained away.

The larger idea is not Fulgorax itself. It is that persistent mechanical motion may be an environmental energy source that biology could, in principle, convert into electricity and then into metabolism.

We do not know whether life can do that.

We now know much more clearly how to find out.
